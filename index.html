    The idea

    Every team has one number. After each game, the winner takes rating points from the loser. How many points move depends on how surprising the result was. Over time, good teams float up and bad teams sink, and the gap between two ratings turns into a win probability.

    This follows the NFL Elo model FiveThirtyEight published from 2014 until 2023.

    1. Starting ratings

    Every team started the 2016 season at 1500. The model then ran through ten full seasons, playoffs included, before this season began. By now the arbitrary starting point has washed out, so the ratings reflect how teams have actually played.

    Between seasons, each rating moves a third of the way back toward 1505. Team quality carries over, but rosters, coaches and luck change, so last year's gaps shrink. A 1650 team becomes about 1602; a 1380 team becomes about 1422.

    2. Win probability

    diff = home Elo + home field − away Elo

    home win % = 1 / (1 + 10^(−diff / 400))

    Home field is worth 48 points (zero at neutral sites). A 100-point edge is about a 64% favorite; 200 points is about 76%.

    3. Rating change after a game

    change = K × margin multiplier × (result − win %)

    Result is 1 for a win, 0 for a loss, ½ for a tie. K is 20. The winner gains exactly what the loser gives up.

    The part in parentheses is the surprise. A 90% favorite that wins only earns 10% of the possible points; an underdog that wins earns most of them.

    margin multiplier = ln(|margin| + 1) × 2.2 / (winner's Elo edge × 0.001 + 2.2)

    Bigger wins count more, but with diminishing returns: winning by 30 is not three times better than winning by 10. The second half dampens blowouts by favorites, so a strong team can't inflate its rating by running up scores on weak opponents.

    Worked example

    Week 1: Denver (1611) at Kansas City (1492).

    diff = 1492 + 48 − 1611 = −71, so the Chiefs' win chance was 40%.

    The Chiefs won 31–10. Margin multiplier = ln(22) × 2.2 / (−0.071 + 2.2) ≈ 3.2.

    Change = 20 × 3.2 × (1 − 0.40) ≈ +38 for Kansas City, −38 for Denver.

    4. Point spread

    Every 25 Elo points is about one point on the scoreboard, rounded to the nearest half. A 290-point edge (including home field) is about an 11.5-point spread.

    How well it works

    Checked against all of 2025: teams the model gave 60–70% won 60% of the time, 70–80% won 71%, and above 80% won 81%. It runs slightly overconfident at the top end, but its numbers have generally held up, and it picked the winner in 183 of 285 games.

    One known weakness: in 2025, home teams won about 8 fewer games than expected, which suggests 48 points of home field may be too much right now. You can test a lower value in Settings.

    What it leaves out

    Quarterbacks and injuries. A team that loses its starter isn't marked down until it starts losing. FiveThirtyEight later added a quarterback adjustment; this model doesn't have one.

    Looking back. Ratings only move forward. If a team you beat turns out to be good, your old win doesn't get re-credited. The error fades as more games are played.

    Early-season noise. Each offseason pulls ratings a third of the way back to average, so the first few weeks lean heavily on last year and are the least reliable.

    Everything else. Weather, rest, travel, and matchups aren't modeled. It sees only who played, where, and the final score.
